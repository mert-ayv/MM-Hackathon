# Bundling: identify the mutual gain

Read which parts and quantities are actually included, what is fixed, and which commitments are
negotiable. A bundle may create useful volume, simplify logistics or fill capacity; establish that
from evidence instead of assuming it from the supplier's category or ownership.

Use the source-backed bundle report to distinguish kits, component pieces and prices per hundred or
thousand. The case's fixed annual kit quantity may already consolidate business previously split
between suppliers; do not assume it offers no volume value simply because it cannot be increased.
Component-price benchmarks are not kit quotations or supplier cost floors. If the BOM or units are
missing, record that uncertainty rather than comparing an unqualified component price with the kit.

Compare the entire horizon against the buyer's actual alternative. Overcommitting volume can cost
more than a discount saves, and a short agreement can leave demand uncovered. Longer contracts and
payment timing are candidate trades when the supplier values them, not mandatory asks.
Review the automatic package and one-field comparisons before confirming. Standard payment terms
are a starting position; assess whether changing them is worth testing. A committee approval threshold
is a process to investigate, not a universal cap on price requests.

Use qualitative questions to discover a better package. Adapt concessions and stopping decisions to
the reply and remaining time; do not assume a particular approval ladder or a fixed concession rate.
